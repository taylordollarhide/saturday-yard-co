His sprinklers were off all summer. He came home to this.

Before — dead on arrival

4 weeks later

He'd been away for most of the summer. When he left, the lawn was fine. When he came back, it was gone — completely brown, dry to the core, with bare patches spreading across the front yard and the boulevard strip. He had no idea what happened until he started asking around.

Turns out the landscaping crew that had been maintaining the property had shut off his irrigation system and never turned it back on. His grass had gone weeks without water during the hottest stretch of the summer. By the time he got home, it looked like a total loss.

He gave me a call.

What we were dealing with

The lawn wasn't dead in the way people usually assume. Grass can go dormant under extreme stress — it pulls back to protect the root system. The question was whether the roots were still viable. In some spots, they were. In others, the damage had gone too deep.

The boulevard strip was the worst of it. Those narrow grass sections between the sidewalk and the curb absorb a lot of heat from the pavement on both sides — they're the first to go in drought conditions and the last to recover. His strip was almost entirely gone.

What we found

Total irrigation shutoff for 6–8 weeks during peak summer heat. Soil had become hydrophobic in several areas — meaning it had dried to the point where water beads off the surface instead of soaking in. The roots in the hardest-hit zones were completely desiccated.

The treatment approach

The first visit was about getting the soil ready to accept water again — not just turning the sprinklers back on and hoping. When soil goes hydrophobic, irrigation alone won't fix it. Water runs off the surface or channels through cracks without ever reaching the root zone.

  • Soil surfactant application. We applied a wetting agent across the entire lawn to break the hydrophobic layer and allow water to penetrate evenly. This is the step most people skip — and it's why their lawns stall out in recovery.
  • Root revitalization spray. A blend of humic acid, seaweed extract, and bioactive compounds to wake up stressed root systems and accelerate recovery where viable roots still existed.
  • Consistent deep irrigation. Once the soil could absorb it, we got the sprinkler system back on a proper schedule — deep cycles every few days to push moisture down to the root zone, not shallow daily sprays.

We didn't reseed immediately. The first priority was finding out what the existing grass could do on its own. Overseeding too early into stressed soil is wasted seed. We gave the treatment 3–4 weeks to work before making any call on bare spots.

The results

Four weeks out, the lawn had made a serious comeback. The front yard filled back in dramatically — the root system had more life in it than the surface suggested. The boulevard strip, predictably, was the slowest to recover, but even that came back to a condition we wouldn't have bet on after the initial assessment.

What this tells us about irrigation shutoffs

This is a more common situation than most people realize. Landscaping crews come and go, service changes happen, systems get accidentally shut off — and without someone watching the lawn, it can be weeks before anyone notices. By then, the damage is done.

Grass can survive a lot. But weeks without water in July and August in Colorado is past the threshold for most lawns. The good news is that "dead-looking" doesn't always mean dead. If the root system still has something left, the right treatment at the right time can bring it back.

The earlier you catch it, the better the odds. If your lawn is looking off and you're not sure why — don't wait.
